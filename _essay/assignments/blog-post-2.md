My research interests reside in the study of early modern Italian art and art history, that being those works of art created during the late 14th to the end of the 16th centuries. Among these works from this region and within this timeframe, I specifically focus on those which include the artistic portrayal of figures of African descent. Along with artistic analysis, I also conduct historical research on the lives of African persons in early modern Italy and examine how their existence and the ways in which they were perceived impacted the ways in which they are represented by artists within their works (or vice versa). The subfield of Africans in early modern Italian art, situated within the larger fields of Africans in early modern European Art, and the field of early modern Italian/European art as a whole, is a budding new subfield which began in the 1950s and has received considerable scholarly attention within the last two decades.
Within the Digital Humanities certificate program, my research project is to create an accessible digital database both for my own and other scholar's research but also open to the public of several of these works, links to select academic articles related to both the work of art and artist who portrayed it and organized by city-state on the Italian peninsula. At this point, I am aware of two databases dedicated to Africans in European art, the Menil Collection in Texas and the Warburg Institute in the UK. Both have online databases; however, the latter, in particular, though immense is mostly in black and white (given that distinct physiognomic signifiers are often not employed in several works, makes it quite difficult without considerable scholarly knowledge on the subject to parse out African figures at times) and thus is especially not accessible to the average viewer. In addition, the other canon is that of The Image of the Black in Western Art, the multi-volume Harvard publication which is an excellent survey and includes a multitude of images, but again, due to cost and the generalization in the way in which it is presented does not lend itself to more pointed examinations.
Some of the ways in which I intend to incorporate digital methods into my own personal research include those which aid in organizing visual works and searching and identifying relevant artworks for further research. The use of digital archival resources and platforms will allow for categorization of works I am currently researching, or even the creation of a broader canon for me to review works portraying artistic depictions of Africans in early modern art. Such will not only aid concepts which I would be analyzing at the moment but also allow for close examinations and comparisons thus leading to future research questions or new realizations for later exploration.
	In further regard to future examinations, resources such as the Distant Viewing Explorer will make reviewing online databases significantly easier and more thorough. When conducting an art historical argument, there can never be a shortage of works needed to support your position. When examining online databases of thousands of visual works, especially those in black and white, it can not only be extraordinarily time consuming but also runs the risk of potentially missing a crucial work of art that may be consequential either in favor or even against one's point. This and similar tools can help increase the speed of this process while also helping to lessen this chance of missing such works. In addition to tools to assist in procuring or organizing works for further research, the creation of an online webpage can serve to make my own research, and that of the subfield of Africans in Italian Renaissance art, more accessible to a wider audience.
