Each of these works prominently feature a representation of African figures in early modern Italian art with ties to themes of political and social power and authority. Many representations of Africans in historical representations, academic discourse, and societal beliefs tie them to themes of bondage, servitude, and discontent; however, in these works, I show the complex and perpetuated tie between Africanity and authority. Though such a theme cannot be fully explored in a measly five works, I aim to highlight a few of the main components of such an intriguing and intertwined history with a focus on those works from the acclaimed Italian Renaissance.  
	Beginning with the first, that being a portrait of a, likely and as generally agreed upon by majority of scholars, to have been an Italian duke of African descent, Duke Alessandro de Medici. A figure who ruled briefly over the city state of Florence and a member of the esteemed Medici family, credited for sponsoring many of the prominent artists in the history of the Italian Renaissance, Alessandro served as the first of many in the new "duchy" of Florence. The second "portrait" is actually a representation and symbol for another prominent Florentine family, the Pucci. As their coat of arms, they used a series of African profiles and they symbol of an African woman and a lily, a religious symbol of purity. This is similar to the two other symbolic representations of Africans and royalty, the representation of Duke Ludovico Sforza of Milan as an African and Marchioness Isabella d'Este who commissioned a work by Correggio of her represented as Minerva with an African woman next to her. Isabella has a long documented fascination with African peoples and cultures having personally sought out many to be her retainers or entertainment. It was also suggested that the portrayal of Judith with a black maidservant may have been a request by her to the artist Mantengna, with whom she had a close relationship, and given that Judith had not been portrayed with an African servant in prior representations of this Christian scene. As such, from portraits of real royal African figures to allegorical representations tied to elites, the unique and controversial ties between African peoples to Italian aristocracy is clearly present even in these five striking works of art.
